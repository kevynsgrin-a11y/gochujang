Gochujang Bolognese

Prep: 20 minutes · Cook: 2 hours · Total: About 2 hours 20 minutes · Serves 4–6

Ingredients

The ragù

  • 2 tbsp olive oil (about 25 g); 100 g pancetta or thick bacon, diced
  • 1 onion (about 150 g), 1 carrot (about 100 g), 1 celery stalk (about 80 g) — pulsed to a fine soffritto in the processor
  • 500 g ground beef (or half beef, half pork)
  • 2 tbsp tomato paste (about 30 g); **2 tbsp gochujang (about 40 g)**
  • 250 ml dry white wine (or the honest swap: 200 ml water plus a tablespoon of vinegar)
  • 800 g canned whole tomatoes, crushed by hand
  • 150 ml whole milk
  • 1 bay leaf; salt (about 5 g) and pepper
  • 30 g parmesan rind, if the cheese drawer offers one — the braise's umami ally

The pasta and service

  • 400 g tagliatelle or spaghetti
  • 40 g parmesan, grated, plus more at the table; a knob of butter (about 15 g), for the finish

Instructions

  1. The pancetta rendered in the oil until its fat runs and its edges crisp; the soffritto in and softened slowly, 8–10 minutes to sweet and gold — no browning, the Italian law intact. Checkpoint — the soffritto smells sweet-vegetal and the fat is glossed through it — the braise's foundation, identical to the classic's at this point and meant to be.
  2. **The bloom, at the doctrine's hinge:** the tomato paste and the gochujang into the soffritto's fat together, fried and stirred 60–90 seconds until both darken and the pan smells of tomato-caramel with a warm, savory hum beneath it. Checkpoint — the paste melts into the tomato paste's color — one rust-red paste, not two. The pan should smell richer than tomato paste alone, and no one smelling it should say "Korean." That is the whisper, made audible only to the cook.
  3. The ground beef in and broken to a crumble, browned through the paste coating; the wine to scrape the fond, simmering off its raw edge 2 minutes; the tomatoes, the rind, the bay, and salt; then the barest simmer, lid ajar, 1.5–2 hours, stirring each half hour — and the milk stirred in at the last 30 minutes, the traditional finish that rounds the acid. Checkpoint — the ragù's done state: the fat separating in glistening beads at the surface, the sauce darkened to brick-mahogany, the consistency thick enough to coat a spoon and hold — and the taste, spoon-tested: deep tomato-beef sweetness first, the milk's roundness, a savory floor beneath both that the table will not place. Place it yourself, silently: the ferment, two hours into its work.
  4. The pasta pulled a minute shy and finished in the ragù with a ladle of its water; the butter and parmesan through the toss. Checkpoint — the eating test, honestly run with a classic-trained taster at the table: the first comment should be about the depth — "what's in this?" — and the answer, if you choose honesty, is a paste that made the ragù taste more like ragù. The doctrine, served.

Storage, Make-Ahead & Variations

- The doctrine's reward: the ragù keeps 4 days and improves daily, the ferment deepening exactly as the classic's does — the make-ahead Sunday pot that answers five weeknights. - Variations: the birria crossover (a spoon of #10's beef into the ragù's logic — the corpus's own loop); a dial-2 nudge with a pinch of gochugaru for the heat-honest table; the white-sauce winter fork — no tomato, cream and the paste, the other doctrine test.
